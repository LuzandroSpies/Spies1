# Creative finance: a plain-English reference

How creative deals work, what creative investors look for, and how to do them safely.
Written for DealFlip users. This is not legal or tax advice: laws differ by state, so have a
local real estate attorney check your documents before you use them.

## Why creative finance exists

Some sellers can't or won't sell for a cash price:

- they owe close to what the house is worth, so a cash sale leaves them nothing;
- they're behind on payments and need it solved fast;
- they own the house outright and would rather be paid over time.

Creative investors buy these houses on **terms** instead of with a big cash price. When
interest rates are high, a seller's old low-rate loan is valuable to an investor.

## The main structures

| Structure | What happens | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject-to** ("sub-to") | The investor takes over the seller's monthly mortgage payments. The deed goes to the investor; the loan stays in the seller's name. | Sellers with a loan and little equity, especially low-rate loans |
| **Seller finance** (owner finance) | The seller acts as the bank: the investor pays a down payment and then monthly payments to the seller. | Houses that are paid off, or sellers who want monthly income |
| **Hybrid** | Subject-to on the existing loan, plus the seller carries a second loan for their equity. | Sellers with a loan and real equity |
| **Wraparound** ("wrap") | The investor resells on seller finance to an end buyer while the original loan stays in place underneath. | Investors exiting to owner-occupants. Heavily regulated, see below |
| **Lease option** | The investor leases with an option to buy later at a set price. | Sellers who aren't ready to sell yet |
| **Novation** | You partner with the seller to fix up and list the house, splitting the gain. | Houses with equity that need work, sold retail |

Pace Morby (SubTo community) popularised the subject-to plus seller-finance approach. The
"Morby Method" is described as combining a new investor loan for part of the price with the
seller carrying the rest. The "Gator" approach is short-term private lending to fund the cash
parts of these deals. Caleb Christopher (Creative TC) focuses on structuring and closing these
deals so they are safe, legal and clearly understood by everyone involved.

## How you (the wholesaler) get paid

Same as a cash deal: you sign the seller to a purchase agreement on creative terms, then
assign that contract to a creative investor. Your fee is part of the investor's **entry fee**
and is paid at closing through the title company.

## What creative investors look at

1. **Entry fee**: all the cash they bring to close: cash to the seller, any missed payments
   (arrears), closing and servicing setup costs, and your fee.
2. **Monthly payment**: the existing loan's full payment (principal, interest, taxes and
   insurance, called "PITI") plus any seller-loan payment.
3. **Cash flow**: rent minus the payments and running costs (DealFlip assumes 21% of rent for
   vacancy, maintenance, big repairs and management).
4. **Interest rate**: a 3% loan versus a new loan at ~7% can be worth hundreds a month.
5. **Equity**: value minus everything owed.
6. **Balloons and loan type**: when a seller loan comes due in full, and whether the existing
   loan is FHA, VA or conventional.

Ask each investor for their limits ("most cash you'll bring" and "least monthly cash flow")
and DealFlip will match deals to them.

## Information to collect from the seller

- A recent mortgage statement (lender, loan number, balance, rate, payment, any escrow)
- Whether they're behind, and by how much (a reinstatement quote from the lender)
- Loan type (FHA, VA, conventional, USDA) and any second mortgages, liens or HOA dues
- What they need in cash, and when they need to move
- Signed authorization so the lender can talk to you and the title company

## Risks, and how legitimate operators handle them

- **Due-on-sale clause.** Almost every mortgage lets the lender demand full payment if the
  house is sold. The Garn-St Germain Act blocks this only for narrow transfers (to family, to
  a living trust the borrower stays in, divorce, death), not an investor purchase. Lenders
  often don't act on it, but they can. The seller must understand this in writing.
- **The seller's credit and the loan stay exposed.** If the investor stops paying, the
  seller's credit is damaged and the house can be foreclosed. Good practice: a third-party
  **loan servicing company** so the seller can see each payment, insurance naming the lender,
  and a written default plan (for example, the deed comes back to the seller if payments stop).
- **VA loans.** The seller's VA benefit stays tied to the house until the loan is paid off.
- **Sellers in foreclosure.** Several states have "foreclosure rescue" or "equity purchaser"
  laws (Florida §501.1377 is one example) with required written terms, cancellation rights
  and limits on buy-back deals. Some states effectively ban certain transactions with owners
  in default. Check your state before contracting with a seller who is behind.
- **Seller financing rules.** Selling a home to someone who will live in it on seller finance
  can bring in federal lending rules (Dodd-Frank, the SAFE Act) and may require a licensed
  loan originator. Sales to investors for business use are generally treated differently, but
  confirm with an attorney, especially for wraps.
- **Wraps and contracts for deed.** Some states (Texas is a well-known example) have strict
  rules for these. Get local legal advice before doing them.
- **Title.** Use a title company experienced with creative deals, and don't close without
  clear title.

## Ethics, in one line

Explain everything plainly, put every risk in writing for the seller to initial, never rush
a seller who is behind on payments, and suggest they get independent advice. It protects the
seller, and it protects you.

## In DealFlip

On any deal, step 1 has a switch: **How will the investor buy? With cash | Creative terms**.
Creative terms asks for the seller's loan details and shows the investor's entry fee, monthly
payment, rent and cash flow. Investors are matched by their entry-fee and cash-flow limits,
and the purchase agreement, assignment agreement, offer letter and investor email all switch
to creative versions, including the seller disclosures above.

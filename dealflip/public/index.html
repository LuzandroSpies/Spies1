      How creative deals work
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
      Some sellers can't sell for cash: they owe too much, or they'd rather get paid over time. Creative investors buy those houses on terms instead of with a big cash price.
      
      
      The three main ways
      
      
      Subject-to. The investor takes over the seller's monthly mortgage payment. The loan stays in the seller's name; the house goes in the investor's name. Investors love a low old interest rate, like 3%.
      
      
      Seller finance. The seller acts like the bank: the investor pays the seller each month instead of paying cash up front. Best for paid-off houses.
      
      
      Both (hybrid). Take over the existing loan, and the seller carries a second loan for their equity.
      
      
      What investors look at
      
      
      Entry fee: all the cash they bring to close: cash to the seller, any missed payments, closing costs, and your fee.
      Cash flow: rent minus the payments and running costs, each month.
      Equity: what the house is worth minus everything owed.
      
      
      How you get paid
      
      
      Same as before: you sign the seller up on creative terms, then assign the contract to a creative investor. Your fee is part of their entry fee and is paid at closing.
      
      
      Protect the seller (and yourself)
      
      
      The loan stays in the seller's name, so if the investor stops paying, the seller's credit suffers. The lender's due-on-sale clause also lets it demand the whole balance after a sale. Legitimate operators put every risk in writing for the seller to initial, use a loan servicing company so the seller can see every payment, close with a title company that knows creative deals, and never rush a seller who is behind on payments. Several states have strict rules for buying from owners in foreclosure. Have a local attorney check your documents.
